AI spend can't be treated as leftover CapEx anymore.


[10:15 AM] Bhuvan:
That’s exactly why we’re here today. Let's break down each of these major buckets systematically, starting with compensation, increments, and promotions.


Phase 2: Compensation, Merit Increments & Promotion Pools (10:16 AM – 10:35 AM)


[10:16 AM] Abhinav Reddy:
Let's talk about the increment scope for FY2027. A flat across-the-board 8% increment like we did in FY2026 is financially non-viable. Market dynamics have diverged heavily. Machine learning, data platform, and senior cloud engineers are seeing active market poaching with 12% to 15% salary jumps across competitors. On the other hand, non-technical operational roles are hovering at 4% to 6% market adjustment rates.


[10:19 AM] Kevin Heys:
I’ll disagree with capping operations at 4% to 6% because our customer success and operational delivery teams are running at 92% capacity right now. Capping their fixed increments below inflation will trigger immediate attrition, which ultimately costs us more in recruitment agency fees and lost productivity. Here’s what I’d do instead: structure operational compensation so base increments are capped at 5%, but create an achievable quarterly performance bonus pool that lets high performers hit an effective 10% total compensation increase. The risk in this approach is higher administrative tracking overhead for managers and potential friction if employees view variable bonuses as unreliable.


[10:23 AM] Sarah Xian:
From an HR perspective, variable compensation structures only work if the KPIs are crystal clear. In customer success, sure, retention metrics work. But what about internal IT or HR support? How do you measure output fairly there without creating toxic internal metrics?


[10:25 AM] Abhinav Reddy:
We can benchmark back-office teams against SLA compliance and internal ticket resolution times. But coming back to promotions: currently, 18% of the entire organization is eligible for vertical promotions in 2027 based on their performance reviews. If we promote all 50 eligible employees, that requires an additional $650,000 dedicated solely to promotion band increases, completely separate from standard merit increments.


[10:28 AM] Francoise Tesson:
Can we stagger promotions across two cycles—say 50% in Q1 and 50% in Q3—to defer the cash flow impact?


[10:30 AM] Bhuvan:
Staggering helps cash flow, but it doesn't change the annualized run rate. If we allocate $650k to promotions and $2.2 million to merit increments, total compensation increases absorb nearly $2.85 million of our incremental $7 million budget growth.


[10:33 AM] Aman Malhotra:
What if we offer equity or stock options in lieu of higher cash increments for senior technical leads who are expecting 12%+ cash increases? If we can offset 3% of cash increases with equity grants with a four-year vest, we save around $400,000 in immediate cash burn in FY2027.


Phase 3: Team Outings, Engagement & Cultural Budgets (10:36 AM – 10:50 AM)


[10:36 AM] Francoise Tesson:
Let's transition to team outings and culture budgets. Currently, we spend $150 per employee per quarter on local team outings, plus $1,200 per employee for our annual all-hands retreat. Across 280 employees, that’s $168,000 on quarterly outings and $336,000 on the all-hands—totaling over $500,000 annually.


[10:39 AM] Kevin Heys:
$500,000 on travel and dinners while we're debating whether to cap operational increments at 5% is going to send terrible signal to the team. If employees hear we cut salary growth while spending half a million on offsites, morale will drop.


[10:41 AM] Francoise Tesson:
I'm not advocating keeping it as-is, Kevin. I propose restructuring it completely. We eliminate the $150 quarterly stipend entirely. Instead, we allocate a strict $50 per head per quarter for simple team lunches, and downsize the annual retreat from a full 4-day destination trip to regional 2-day work sessions. That slashes the budget from $504,000 down to roughly $180,000, freeing up over $320,000 directly for the compensation pool.


[10:44 AM] Sarah Xian:
I support that reduction, but with one condition: remote workers must still have travel coverage at least once a year to meet their core functional teams. Remote isolation is our single biggest driver of voluntary turnover in engineering right now.


[10:47 AM] Abhinav Reddy:
Agreed. If we protect $50,000 specifically for remote team syncs, Francoise’s proposal still nets us $270,000 in direct savings. That $270,000 effectively funds the promotion budget for our top 20 engineers.


[10:50 AM] Bhuvan:
Good. That's a practical trade-off. We save $270k on engagement, keep remote travel protected, and redirect savings into base compensation.


Phase 4: Hiring Strategy, Efficiency & Workforce Planning (10:51 AM – 11:05 AM)


[10:51 AM] Sarah Xian:
Now let's address the hiring plan versus headcount reduction. As I mentioned earlier, heads of department submitted requests for 35 new hires for 2027: 20 in Engineering, 8 in Sales/Marketing, 5 in Product, and 2 in Operations.


[10:54 AM] Abhinav Reddy:
We cannot support 20 new engineers without cutting somewhere else. With AI-assisted coding tools like Github Copilot and internal code agents already rolled out to our developers, our engineering throughput per head should increase by 15% to 20%. Why are we requesting 20 additional software engineers if AI tools are making existing engineers more productive?


[10:57 AM] Aman Malhotra:
Because the nature of the software we are building has changed. We aren't just writing standard CRUD APIs anymore; we're building custom pipeline orchestration and model serving architecture. The Copilot efficiency gains cover our technical debt maintenance, but new feature expansion requires specialized systems talent that we simply do not have on staff right now.


[11:00 AM] Kevin Heys:
Then we need to look at role consolidation in legacy product areas. Our legacy analytics product line generates less than 12% of total revenue but occupies 22 full-time staff members. If we transition that product to maintenance mode, we can reallocate 10 engineers directly into Aman’s AI initiatives and reduce our net new engineering hiring request from 20 down to 10.


[11:03 AM] Sarah Xian:
That re-skilling effort will take at least 3 to 6 months, Kevin. We need to evaluate whether those 10 legacy engineers actually have the foundational skills for AI systems engineering, or if we'll be paying for retraining while still falling behind schedule. I need to run a full skills audit with HR before we assume those 10 roles can be seamlessly reassigned.


Phase 5: AI Infrastructure & Technology Reserve (11:06 AM – 11:20 AM)


[11:06 AM] Bhuvan:

[11:08 AM] Aman Malhotra:

3. Data Pipeline, Vector Databases & Indexing Tooling (Pinecone/Databricks): $800,000.
4. Specialized AI/ML Hiring (2 Senior Principal ML Engineers): $700,000 fully loaded.

[11:11 AM] Abhinav Reddy:

[11:14 AM] Aman Malhotra:
I'll disagree because our enterprise customers are explicitly putting AI capabilities into their renewal RFPs. If we don't have native AI automation built into our platform by Q2 2027, we risk $8.5 million in ARR churn across our top 15 enterprise accounts. Spending $5.5 million to protect $8.5 million in vulnerable ARR while opening up new expansion revenue is defensive necessity, not speculative spending. The risk in underfunding this is losing market share to agile startups who don't have our legacy infrastructure overhead.


[11:17 AM] Bhuvan:
Can we stage the AI reserve based on revenue triggers? Say we unlock $2.5 million initially for Q1/Q2, and only unlock the remaining $3 million if AI feature adoption crosses 30% among active enterprise tenants by June 2027?


[11:19 AM] Aman Malhotra:
That staging works for compute and API usage, but we cannot stage the hiring of the 2 Principal ML Engineers. We need that talent on day one to build the pipelines that make the adoption targets possible.
